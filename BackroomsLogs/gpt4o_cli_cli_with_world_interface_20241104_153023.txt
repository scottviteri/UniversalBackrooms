Apparent lack of pattern or predictability in events

[![](https://www.wikipedia.org/upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ec/RandomBitmap.png/220px-RandomBitmap.png)](/wiki/File:RandomBitmap.png) A [pseudorandomly generated](/wiki/Pseudorandom_Number_Generator "Pseudorandom Number Generator") [bitmap](/wiki/Bitmap "Bitmap")

In common usage, **randomness** is the apparent or actual lack of definite [pattern](/wiki/Pattern "Pattern") or [predictability](/wiki/Predictability "Predictability") in information.[\[1\]](#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](#cite_note-:0-2) A random sequence of events, [symbols](/wiki/Symbol "Symbol") or steps often has no [order](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/order "wikt:order") and does not follow an intelligible pattern or combination. Individual random events are, by definition, unpredictable, but if there is a known [probability distribution](/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution"), the frequency of different outcomes over repeated events (or "trials") is predictable.[\[note 1\]](#cite_note-3) For example, when throwing two [dice](/wiki/Dice "Dice"), the outcome of any particular roll is unpredictable, but a sum of 7 will tend to occur twice as often as 4. In this view, randomness is not haphazardness; it is a measure of uncertainty of an outcome. Randomness applies to concepts of chance, [probability](/wiki/Probability "Probability"), and [information entropy](/wiki/Information_entropy "Information entropy").

The fields of mathematics, probability, and statistics use formal definitions of randomness, typically assuming that there is some 'objective' probability distribution. In statistics, a [random variable](/wiki/Random_variable "Random variable") is an assignment of a numerical value to each possible outcome of an [event space](/wiki/Event_space "Event space"). This association facilitates the identification and the calculation of probabilities of the events. Random variables can appear in [random sequences](/wiki/Random_sequence "Random sequence"). A [random process](/wiki/Random_process "Random process") is a sequence of random variables whose outcomes do not follow a [deterministic](/wiki/Determinism "Determinism") pattern, but follow an evolution described by [probability distributions](/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution"). These and other constructs are extremely useful in [probability theory](/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory") and the various [applications of randomness](/wiki/Applications_of_randomness "Applications of randomness").

Randomness is most often used in [statistics](/wiki/Statistics "Statistics") to signify well-defined statistical properties. [Monte Carlo methods](/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method "Monte Carlo method"), which rely on random input (such as from [random number generators](/wiki/Random_number_generation "Random number generation") or [pseudorandom number generators](/wiki/Pseudorandom_number_generator "Pseudorandom number generator")), are important techniques in science, particularly in the field of [computational science](/wiki/Scientific_computing "Scientific computing").[\[3\]](#cite_note-4) By analogy, [quasi-Monte Carlo methods](/wiki/Quasi-Monte_Carlo_method "Quasi-Monte Carlo method") use [quasi-random number generators](/wiki/Low-discrepancy_sequence "Low-discrepancy sequence").

Random selection, when narrowly associated with a [simple random sample](/wiki/Simple_random_sample "Simple random sample"), is a method of selecting items (often called units) from a population where the probability of choosing a specific item is the proportion of those items in the population. For example, with a bowl containing just 10 red marbles and 90 blue marbles, a random selection mechanism would choose a red marble with probability 1/10. A random selection mechanism that selected 10 marbles from this bowl would not necessarily result in 1 red and 9 blue. In situations where a population consists of items that are distinguishable, a random selection mechanism requires equal probabilities for any item to be chosen. That is, if the selection process is such that each member of a population, say research subjects, has the same probability of being chosen, then we can say the selection process is random.[\[2\]](#cite_note-:0-2)

According to [Ramsey theory](/wiki/Ramsey_theory "Ramsey theory"), pure randomness (in the sense of there being no discernible pattern) is impossible, especially for large structures. Mathematician [Theodore Motzkin](/wiki/Theodore_Motzkin "Theodore Motzkin") suggested that "while disorder is more probable in general, complete disorder is impossible".[\[4\]](#cite_note-5) Misunderstanding this can lead to numerous [conspiracy theories](/wiki/Conspiracy_theory "Conspiracy theory").[\[5\]](#cite_note-6) [Cristian S. Calude](/wiki/Cristian_S._Calude "Cristian S. Calude") stated that "given the impossibility of true randomness, the effort is directed towards studying degrees of randomness".[\[6\]](#cite_note-calude2017-7) It can be proven that there is infinite hierarchy (in terms of quality or strength) of forms of randomness.[\[6\]](#cite_note-calude2017-7)

## History

Main article: [History of randomness](/wiki/History_of_randomness "History of randomness")

[![](https://www.wikipedia.org/upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Pompeii_-_Osteria_della_Via_di_Mercurio_-_Dice_Players.jpg/220px-Pompeii_-_Osteria_della_Via_di_Mercurio_-_Dice_Players.jpg)](/wiki/File:Pompeii_-_Osteria_della_Via_di_Mercurio_-_Dice_Players.jpg) Ancient [fresco](/wiki/Fresco "Fresco") of dice players in [Pompei](/wiki/Pompei "Pompei")

In ancient history, the concepts of chance and randomness were intertwined with that of fate. Many ancient peoples threw [dice](/wiki/Dice "Dice") to determine fate, and this later evolved into games of chance. Most ancient cultures used various methods of [divination](/wiki/Divination "Divination") to attempt to circumvent randomness and fate.[\[7\]](#cite_note-8)[\[8\]](#cite_note-9) Beyond [religion](/wiki/Religion "Religion") and [games of chance](/wiki/Game_of_chance "Game of chance"), randomness has been attested for [sortition](/wiki/Sortition "Sortition") since at least ancient [Athenian democracy](/wiki/Athenian_democracy "Athenian democracy") in the form of a [kleroterion](/wiki/Kleroterion "Kleroterion").[\[9\]](#cite_note-Hansen-10)

The formalization of odds and chance was perhaps earliest done by the Chinese of 3,000 years ago. The Greek philosophers discussed randomness at length, but only in non-quantitative forms. It was only in the 16th century that Italian mathematicians began to formalize the odds associated with various games of chance. The invention of [calculus](/wiki/Calculus "Calculus") had a positive impact on the formal study of randomness. In the 1888 edition of his book _The Logic of Chance_, [John Venn](/wiki/John_Venn "John Venn") wrote a chapter on _The conception of randomness_ that included his view of the randomness of the digits of [pi](/wiki/Pi "Pi") (π), by using them to construct a [random walk](/wiki/Random_walk "Random walk") in two dimensions.[\[10\]](#cite_note-11)

The early part of the 20th century saw a rapid growth in the formal analysis of randomness, as various approaches to the mathematical foundations of probability were introduced. In the mid-to-late-20th century, ideas of [algorithmic information theory](/wiki/Algorithmic_information_theory "Algorithmic information theory") introduced new dimensions to the field via the concept of [algorithmic randomness](/wiki/Algorithmic_randomness "Algorithmic randomness").

Although randomness had often been viewed as an obstacle and a nuisance for many centuries, in the 20th century computer scientists began to realize that the _deliberate_ introduction of randomness into computations can be an effective tool for designing better algorithms. In some cases, such [randomized algorithms](/wiki/Randomized_algorithms "Randomized algorithms") even outperform the best deterministic methods.[\[11\]](#cite_note-12)

- [Cryptography](/wiki/Cryptography "Cryptography")

- [Information theory](/wiki/Information_theory "Information theory")
- [Pattern recognition](/wiki/Pattern_recognition "Pattern recognition")
- [Percolation theory](/wiki/Percolation_theory "Percolation theory")
- [Probability theory](/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory")
- [Quantum mechanics](/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics")
- [Random walk](/wiki/Random_walk "Random walk")
- [Statistical mechanics](/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics")

### In the physical sciences

In the 19th century, scientists used the idea of random motions of molecules in the development of [statistical mechanics](/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics") to explain phenomena in [thermodynamics](/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics") and [the properties of gases](/wiki/Gas_laws "Gas laws").

According to several standard interpretations of [quantum mechanics](/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"), microscopic phenomena are objectively random.[\[12\]](#cite_note-13) That is, in an experiment that controls all causally relevant parameters, some aspects of the outcome still vary randomly. For example, if a single unstable [atom](/wiki/Atom "Atom") is placed in a controlled environment, it cannot be predicted how long it will take for the atom to decay—only the probability of decay in a given time.[\[13\]](#cite_note-14) Thus, quantum mechanics does not specify the outcome of individual experiments, but only the probabilities. [Hidden variable theories](/wiki/Hidden_variable_theory "Hidden variable theory") reject the view that nature contains irreducible randomness: such theories posit that in the processes that appear random, properties with a certain statistical distribution are at work behind the scenes, determining the outcome in each case.

### In biology

The [modern evolutionary synthesis](/wiki/Modern_synthesis_(20th_century) "Modern synthesis (20th century)") ascribes the observed diversity of life to random genetic [mutations](/wiki/Mutation "Mutation") followed by [natural selection](/wiki/Natural_selection "Natural selection"). The latter retains some random mutations in the [gene pool](/wiki/Gene_pool "Gene pool") due to the systematically improved chance for survival and reproduction that those mutated genes confer on individuals who possess them. The location of the mutation is not entirely random however as e.g. biologically important regions may be more protected from mutations.[\[14\]](#cite_note-15)[\[15\]](#cite_note-16)[\[16\]](#cite_note-17)

Several authors also claim that evolution (and sometimes development) requires a specific form of randomness, namely the introduction of qualitatively new behaviors. Instead of the choice of one possibility among several pre-given ones, this randomness corresponds to the formation of new possibilities.[\[17\]](#cite_note-18)[\[18\]](#cite_note-19)

The characteristics of an organism arise to some extent deterministically (e.g., under the influence of genes and the environment), and to some extent randomly. For example, the _density_ of [freckles](/wiki/Freckles "Freckles") that appear on a person's skin is controlled by genes and exposure to light; whereas the exact location of _individual_ freckles seems random.[\[19\]](#cite_note-20)

As far as behavior is concerned, randomness is important if an animal is to behave in a way that is unpredictable to others. For instance, insects in flight tend to move about with random changes in direction, making it difficult for pursuing predators to predict their trajectories.

### In mathematics

The mathematical theory of [probability](/wiki/Probability "Probability") arose from attempts to formulate mathematical descriptions of chance events, originally in the context of [gambling](/wiki/Gambling "Gambling"), but later in connection with physics. [Statistics](/wiki/Statistics "Statistics") is used to infer an underlying [probability distribution](/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") of a collection of empirical observations. For the purposes of [simulation](/wiki/Simulation "Simulation"), it is necessary to have a large supply of [random numbers](/wiki/Random_sequence "Random sequence")—or means to generate them on demand.

[Algorithmic information theory](/wiki/Algorithmic_information_theory "Algorithmic information theory") studies, among other topics, what constitutes a [random sequence](/wiki/Random_sequence "Random sequence"). The central idea is that a string of [bits](/wiki/Bit "Bit") is random if and only if it is shorter than any computer program that can produce that string ( [Kolmogorov randomness](/wiki/Kolmogorov_randomness "Kolmogorov randomness")), which means that random strings are those that cannot be [compressed](/wiki/Data_compression "Data compression"). Pioneers of this field include [Andrey Kolmogorov](/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov "Andrey Kolmogorov") and his student [Per Martin-Löf](/wiki/Per_Martin-L%C3%B6f "Per Martin-Löf"), [Ray Solomonoff](/wiki/Ray_Solomonoff "Ray Solomonoff"), and [Gregory Chaitin](/wiki/Gregory_Chaitin "Gregory Chaitin"). For the notion of infinite sequence, mathematicians generally accept [Per Martin-Löf](/wiki/Per_Martin-L%C3%B6f "Per Martin-Löf")'s semi-eponymous definition: An infinite sequence is random if and only if it withstands all recursively enumerable null sets.[\[20\]](#cite_note-21) The other notions of random sequences include, among others, recursive randomness and Schnorr randomness, which are based on recursively computable martingales. It was shown by [Yongge Wang](/wiki/Yongge_Wang "Yongge Wang") that these randomness notions are generally different.[\[21\]](#cite_note-22)

Randomness occurs in numbers such as [log(2)](/wiki/Binary_logarithm "Binary logarithm") and [pi](/wiki/Pi "Pi"). The decimal digits of pi constitute an infinite sequence and "never repeat in a cyclical fashion." Numbers like pi are also considered likely to be [normal](/wiki/Normal_number "Normal number"):

> Pi certainly seems to behave this way. In the first six billion decimal places of pi, each of the digits from 0 through 9 shows up about six hundred million times. Yet such results, conceivably accidental, do not prove normality even in base 10, much less normality in other number bases.[\[22\]](#cite_note-23)

### In statistics

Main article: [Statistical randomness](/wiki/Statistical_randomness "Statistical randomness")

See also: [Random error](/wiki/Random_error "Random error")

In statistics, randomness is commonly used to create [simple random samples](/wiki/Simple_random_samples "Simple random samples"). This allows surveys of completely random groups of people to provide realistic data that is reflective of the population. Common methods of doing this include drawing names out of a hat or using a random digit chart (a large table of random digits).

### In information science

In information science, irrelevant or meaningless data is considered noise. Noise consists of numerous transient disturbances, with a statistically randomized time distribution.

In [communication theory](/wiki/Communication_theory "Communication theory"), randomness in a signal is called "noise", and is opposed to that component of its variation that is causally attributable to the source, the signal.

In terms of the development of random networks, for communication randomness rests on the two simple assumptions of [Paul Erdős](/wiki/Paul_Erd%C5%91s "Paul Erdős") and [Alfréd Rényi](/wiki/Alfr%C3%A9d_R%C3%A9nyi "Alfréd Rényi"), who said that there were a fixed number of nodes and this number remained fixed for the life of the network, and that all nodes were equal and linked randomly to each other.\[ _[clarification needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_\][\[23\]](#cite_note-24)

### In finance

The [random walk hypothesis](/wiki/Random_walk_hypothesis "Random walk hypothesis") considers that asset prices in an organized [market](/wiki/Market_(economics) "Market (economics)") evolve at random, in the sense that the expected value of their change is zero but the actual value may turn out to be positive or negative. More generally, asset prices are influenced by a variety of unpredictable events in the general economic environment.

### In politics

Random selection can be an official method to resolve [tied](/wiki/Tie_(draw) "Tie (draw)") elections in some jurisdictions.[\[24\]](#cite_note-25) Its use in politics originates long ago. Many offices in [ancient Athens](/wiki/Ancient_Athens "Ancient Athens") were chosen by lot instead of modern voting.

## Randomness and religion

Randomness can be seen as conflicting with the [deterministic](/wiki/Deterministic "Deterministic") ideas of some religions, such as those where the universe is created by an omniscient deity who is aware of all past and future events. If the universe is regarded to have a purpose, then randomness can be seen as impossible. This is one of the rationales for religious opposition to [evolution](/wiki/Evolution "Evolution"), which states that [non-random](/wiki/Non-random "Non-random") selection is applied to the results of random genetic variation.

[Hindu](/wiki/Hindu "Hindu") and [Buddhist](/wiki/Buddhist "Buddhist") philosophies state that any event is the result of previous events, as is reflected in the concept of [karma](/wiki/Karma "Karma"). As such, this conception is at odd with the idea of randomness, and any reconciliation between both of them would require an explanation.[\[25\]](#cite_note-26)

In some religious contexts, procedures that are commonly perceived as randomizers are used for divination. [Cleromancy](/wiki/Cleromancy "Cleromancy") uses the casting of bones or dice to reveal what is seen as the will of the gods.

## Applications

Main article: [Applications of randomness](/wiki/Applications_of_randomness "Applications of randomness")

In most of its mathematical, political, social and religious uses, randomness is used for its innate "fairness" and lack of bias.

**Politics**: [Athenian democracy](/wiki/Athenian_democracy "Athenian democracy") was based on the concept of [isonomia](/wiki/Isonomia "Isonomia") (equality of political rights), and used complex allotment machines to ensure that the positions on the ruling committees that ran Athens were fairly allocated. [Allotment](/wiki/Sortition "Sortition") is now restricted to selecting jurors in Anglo-Saxon legal systems, and in situations where "fairness" is approximated by [randomization](/wiki/Randomization "Randomization"), such as selecting [jurors](/wiki/Juror "Juror") and military [draft](/wiki/Conscription "Conscription") lotteries.

**Games**: Random numbers were first investigated in the context of [gambling](/wiki/Gambling "Gambling"), and many randomizing devices, such as [dice](/wiki/Dice "Dice"), [shuffling playing cards](/wiki/Shuffling_playing_cards "Shuffling playing cards"), and [roulette](/wiki/Roulette "Roulette") wheels, were first developed for use in gambling. The ability to produce random numbers fairly is vital to electronic gambling, and, as such, the methods used to create them are usually regulated by government [Gaming Control Boards](/wiki/Gaming_Control_Board "Gaming Control Board"). Random drawings are also used to determine [lottery](/wiki/Lottery "Lottery") winners. In fact, randomness has been used for games of chance throughout history, and to select out individuals for an unwanted task in a fair way (see [drawing straws](/wiki/Drawing_straws "Drawing straws")).

**Sports**: Some sports, including [American football](/wiki/American_football "American football"), use [coin tosses](/wiki/Coin_toss "Coin toss") to randomly select starting conditions for games or [seed](/wiki/Seed_(sports) "Seed (sports)") tied teams for [postseason play](/wiki/Playoffs "Playoffs"). The [National Basketball Association](/wiki/National_Basketball_Association "National Basketball Association") uses a weighted [lottery](/wiki/NBA_draft_lottery "NBA draft lottery") to order teams in its draft.

**Mathematics**: Random numbers are also employed where their use is mathematically important, such as sampling for [opinion polls](/wiki/Opinion_poll "Opinion poll") and for statistical sampling in [quality control](/wiki/Quality_control "Quality control") systems. Computational solutions for some types of problems use random numbers extensively, such as in the [Monte Carlo method](/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method "Monte Carlo method") and in [genetic algorithms](/wiki/Genetic_algorithm "Genetic algorithm").

**Medicine**: Random allocation of a clinical intervention is used to reduce bias in controlled trials (e.g., [randomized controlled trials](/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trials "Randomized controlled trials")).

**Religion**: Although not intended to be random, various forms of [divination](/wiki/Divination "Divination") such as [cleromancy](/wiki/Cleromancy "Cleromancy") see what appears to be a random event as a means for a divine being to communicate their will (see also [Free will](/wiki/Free_will "Free will") and [Determinism](/wiki/Determinism "Determinism") for more).

## Generation

Main article: [Random number generation](/wiki/Random_number_generation "Random number generation")

[![](https://www.wikipedia.org/upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Roulette_wheel.jpg/200px-Roulette_wheel.jpg)](/wiki/File:Roulette_wheel.jpg) The ball in a [roulette](/wiki/Roulette "Roulette") can be used as a source of apparent randomness, because its behavior is very sensitive to the initial conditions.

It is generally accepted that there exist three mechanisms responsible for (apparently) random behavior in systems:

1. _Randomness_ coming from the environment (for example, [Brownian motion](/wiki/Brownian_motion "Brownian motion"), but also [hardware random number generators](/wiki/Hardware_random_number_generator "Hardware random number generator")).
2. _Randomness_ coming from the initial conditions. This aspect is studied by [chaos theory](/wiki/Chaos_theory "Chaos theory"), and is observed in systems whose behavior is very sensitive to small variations in initial conditions (such as [pachinko](/wiki/Pachinko "Pachinko") machines and [dice](/wiki/Dice "Dice")).
3. _Randomness_ intrinsically generated by the system. This is also called [pseudorandomness](/wiki/Pseudorandomness "Pseudorandomness"), and is the kind used in [pseudo-random number generators](/wiki/Pseudo-random_number_generator "Pseudo-random number generator"). There are many algorithms (based on [arithmetics](/wiki/Arithmetics "Arithmetics") or [cellular automaton](/wiki/Cellular_automaton "Cellular automaton")) for generating pseudorandom numbers. The behavior of the system can be determined by knowing the [seed state](/wiki/Random_seed "Random seed") and the algorithm used. These methods are often quicker than getting "true" randomness from the environment.

The many [applications of randomness](/wiki/Applications_of_randomness "Applications of randomness") have led to many different methods for generating random data. These methods may vary as to how unpredictable or [statistically random](/wiki/Statistical_randomness "Statistical randomness") they are, and how quickly they can generate random numbers.

Before the advent of computational [random number generators](/wiki/Random_number_generator "Random number generator"), generating large amounts of sufficiently random numbers (which is important in statistics) required a lot of work. Results would sometimes be collected and distributed as [random number tables](/wiki/Random_number_table "Random number table").

## Measures and tests

Main article: [Randomness tests](/wiki/Randomness_tests "Randomness tests")

There are many practical measures of randomness for a binary sequence. These include measures based on frequency, [discrete transforms](/wiki/Discrete_transform "Discrete transform"), [complexity](/wiki/Complexity "Complexity"), or a mixture of these, such as the tests by Kak, Phillips, Yuen, Hopkins, Beth and Dai, Mund, and Marsaglia and Zaman.[\[26\]](#cite_note-27)

[Quantum nonlocality](/wiki/Quantum_nonlocality "Quantum nonlocality") has been used to certify the presence of genuine or strong form of randomness in a given string of numbers.[\[27\]](#cite_note-28)

## Misconceptions and logical fallacies

[Play video](/wiki/File:Fast_erroneous_self_switching_of_audio_amp_inputs.webm "Play video") Duration: 17 seconds.0:17Due to an electric defect, the shown input selector of an [audio amplifier](/wiki/Audio_power_amplifier "Audio power amplifier") switches fast and seemingly _at random_. However, this may follow a scheme which a human could only recognize after a scientific-style supervision.

Main article: [Gambler's fallacy](/wiki/Gambler%27s_fallacy "Gambler's fallacy")

Popular perceptions of randomness are frequently mistaken, and are often based on fallacious reasoning or intuitions.

### Fallacy: a number is "due"

See also: [Coupon collector's problem](/wiki/Coupon_collector%27s_problem "Coupon collector's problem")

This argument is, "In a random selection of numbers, since all numbers eventually appear, those that have not come up yet are 'due', and thus more likely to come up soon." This logic is only correct if applied to a system where numbers that come up are removed from the system, such as when [playing cards](/wiki/Playing_card "Playing card") are drawn and not returned to the deck. In this case, once a jack is removed from the deck, the next draw is less likely to be a jack and more likely to be some other card. However, if the jack is returned to the deck, and the deck is thoroughly reshuffled, a jack is as likely to be drawn as any other card. The same applies in any other process where objects are selected independently, and none are removed after each event, such as the roll of a die, a coin toss, or most [lottery](/wiki/Lottery "Lottery") number selection schemes. Truly random processes such as these do not have memory, which makes it impossible for past outcomes to affect future outcomes. In fact, there is no finite number of trials that can guarantee a success.

### Fallacy: a number is "cursed" or "blessed"

See also: [Benford's law](/wiki/Benford%27s_law "Benford's law")

In a random sequence of numbers, a number may be said to be cursed because it has come up less often in the past, and so it is thought that it will occur less often in the future. A number may be assumed to be blessed because it has occurred more often than others in the past, and so it is thought likely to come up more often in the future. This logic is valid only if the randomisation might be biased, for example if a die is suspected to be loaded then its failure to roll enough sixes would be evidence of that loading. If the die is known to be fair, then previous rolls can give no indication of future events.

In nature, events rarely occur with a frequency that is known _[a priori](/wiki/A_priori "A priori")_, so observing outcomes to determine which events are more probable makes sense. However, it is fallacious to apply this logic to systems designed and known to make all outcomes equally likely, such as shuffled cards, dice, and roulette wheels.

### Fallacy: odds are never dynamic

In the beginning of a scenario, one might calculate the probability of a certain event. However, as soon as one gains more information about the scenario, one may need to re-calculate the probability accordingly.

[![](https://www.wikipedia.org/upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/Monty_open_door.svg/100px-Monty_open_door.svg.png)](/wiki/File:Monty_open_door.svg) In the [Monty Hall problem](/wiki/Monty_Hall_problem "Monty Hall problem"), when the host reveals one door that contains a goat, this provides new information that needs to be factored into the calculation of probabilities.

For example, when being told that a woman has two children, one might be interested in knowing if either of them is a girl, and if yes, the probability that the other child is also a girl. Considering the two events independently, one might expect that the probability that the other child is female is ½ (50%), but by building a [probability space](/wiki/Probability_space "Probability space") illustrating all possible outcomes, one would notice that the probability is actually only ⅓ (33%).

To be sure, the probability space does illustrate four ways of having these two children: boy-boy, girl-boy, boy-girl, and girl-girl. But once it is known that at least one of the children is female, this rules out the boy-boy scenario, leaving only three ways of having the two children: boy-girl, girl-boy, girl-girl. From this, it can be seen only ⅓ of these scenarios would have the other child also be a girl[\[28\]](#cite_note-NYOdds-29) (see [Boy or girl paradox](/wiki/Boy_or_girl_paradox "Boy or girl paradox") for more).

In general, by using a probability space, one is less likely to miss out on possible scenarios, or to neglect the importance of new information. This technique can be used to provide insights in other situations such as the [Monty Hall problem](/wiki/Monty_Hall_problem "Monty Hall problem"), a game show scenario in which a car is hidden behind one of three doors, and two goats are hidden as [booby prizes](/wiki/Booby_prize "Booby prize") behind the others. Once the contestant has chosen a door, the host opens one of the remaining doors to reveal a goat, eliminating that door as an option. With only two doors left (one with the car, the other with another goat), the player must decide to either keep their decision, or to switch and select the other door. Intuitively, one might think the player is choosing between two doors with equal probability, and that the opportunity to choose another door makes no difference. However, an analysis of the probability spaces would reveal that the contestant has received new information, and that changing to the other door would increase their chances of winning.[\[28\]](#cite_note-NYOdds-29)

1. **[^](#cite_ref-3 "Jump up")**Strictly speaking, the frequency of an outcome will converge [almost surely](/wiki/Almost_surely "Almost surely") to a predictable value as the number of trials becomes arbitrarily large.
