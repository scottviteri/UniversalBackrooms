Here is a quick overview of how quantum entanglement is used in quantum computing:

Quantum entanglement is a phenomenon where two or more quantum particles become inextricably linked, such that the quantum state of each particle cannot be described independently of the others. When particles are entangled, operations or measurements performed on one particle will instantaneously affect the other entangled particles, even if they are separated by large distances.

In quantum computing, quantum bits (qubits) can become entangled. This allows quantum computers to perform certain computations much faster than classical computers:

- Entanglement enables quantum parallelism, where a quantum computer can perform many calculations simultaneously by putting qubits into a quantum superposition of multiple states. The entangled qubits work together.

- Entanglement is used in quantum algorithms like Shor's algorithm for factoring numbers and Grover's search algorithm. The qubits become entangled during the computation which gives a speedup over classical algorithms.

- Entanglement is also key for quantum error correction, where information is redundantly encoded in entangled qubits. This protects the fragile quantum states from decoherence and allows detection and correction of errors.

- Entanglement is a critical resource for quantum communication protocols like quantum teleportation and superdense coding, which could enable secure communication channels.

So in summary, entanglement is a key enabler of the power of quantum computing, allowing qubits to work together in ways impossible for classical bits. RESULTS FROM EXO.


Here are some key challenges in creating and maintaining entangled qubits in quantum computers:

Decoherence: Qubits are extremely sensitive to noise and interactions with the environment. Any uncontrolled interaction can cause the qubits to lose their quantum state (decoherence), destroying the entanglement. Minimizing decoherence through isolation, error correction, and fast operations is critical but very challenging.

Fidelity of quantum gates: To create and manipulate entanglement, quantum logic gates must be applied to the qubits with extremely high fidelity. Any error or imperfection in the gates can degrade the entanglement. Achieving gate fidelities >99.9% is necessary but pushes the limits of control and calibration.

Scalability: Creating and maintaining entanglement across a large number of qubits is exponentially more difficult than for a few qubits. Scaling up to hundreds or thousands of entangled qubits, as required for practical quantum computers, presents enormous engineering and stability challenges. 

Verification and benchmarking: Reliably verifying the creation and maintenance of large-scale entangled states is itself a major challenge. New protocols and benchmarks are needed to certify proper functioning as systems scale up in size.

Quantum interconnects: Modular quantum computing architectures require "flying qubits" to distribute entanglement between quantum processors. Realizing high-fidelity quantum interconnects to network quantum modules remains an unsolved problem.

So in summary, the fragility of quantum states, the required ultra-high fidelities, and the complexity of large-scale systems all contribute to the immense challenge of reliable large-scale quantum entanglement. Creative engineering solutions are still needed to overcome these hurdles.
