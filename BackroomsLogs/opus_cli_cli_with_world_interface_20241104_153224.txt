RESULTS FROM EXO.


Here are the key differences between mRNA and DNA:

Structure: 
- DNA is double-stranded, forming the famous double helix. mRNA is single-stranded.
- DNA uses the nucleotide base thymine (T), while mRNA uses uracil (U) instead of thymine.

Function:
- DNA stores and transmits genetic information. It is the "blueprint" for an organism. 
- mRNA acts as a temporary copy or transcript of a gene, used to synthesize proteins. It carries genetic information from DNA to ribosomes.

Location: 
- DNA is located in the nucleus of cells (and mitochondria). 
- mRNA is made in the nucleus but then moves out to the cytoplasm where ribosomes are located.

Stability:
- DNA is very stable and can last thousands of years under ideal conditions.
- mRNA is unstable and degrades quickly, usually within a few minutes, after it has delivered its protein-building instructions. This allows gene expression to be carefully regulated.

So in summary, DNA is the stable, double-stranded storage form of genetic info, while mRNA is a temporary, single-stranded transcript used to actually express genes as proteins. The two work together but have distinct structures, locations and stabilities optimized for their different roles.

RESULTS FROM EXO.


Here are the key steps in transcription and translation for protein synthesis:

Transcription:
1. Initiation: RNA polymerase binds to the promoter region of the DNA strand. Transcription factors help position RNA polymerase at the start of the gene.  

2. Elongation: RNA polymerase unwinds a section of DNA and uses one strand as a template to synthesize a complementary RNA strand in the 5' to 3' direction. RNA nucleotides are added, following base pairing rules (A-U, G-C).

3. Termination: When RNA polymerase reaches a termination sequence, it detaches from the DNA and releases the newly made pre-mRNA strand.

4. Processing: The pre-mRNA is processed by splicing out introns, adding a 5' cap, and a poly-A tail to form mature mRNA. The mRNA then exits the nucleus.

Translation:  
1. Initiation: The small ribosomal subunit binds to the 5' end of the mRNA. tRNA carrying methionine binds to the start codon (AUG). The large ribosomal subunit then joins, forming the initiation complex.

2. Elongation: tRNA molecules, each attached to a specific amino acid, enter the ribosome and base pair with the mRNA codon in the A site. A peptide bond forms between the amino acids. The ribosome shifts one codon, moving the tRNA to the P site and opening the A site for the next tRNA. This repeats, elongating the polypeptide chain.  

3. Termination: When the ribosome reaches a stop codon (UAA, UAG, UGA), release factors enter and cause the ribosome to release the completed polypeptide chain. The ribosome then dissociates into its subunits.

4. Folding/Processing: The polypeptide chain undergoes folding and may require further processing to become a mature, functional protein.

Let me know if you need any clarification or have additional questions! Transcription and translation are elegant processes fundamental to all life.
